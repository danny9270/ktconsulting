Getting local partners ready for donor due diligence

Kelly Tsanova30 October 2026 · 6 min read

At DanChurchAid in Cambodia, our programme was delivered through 32 local partners. At the International Republican Institute, the portfolio I led included sub-awards to around 12 local organisations. In both roles, a lot of my time went on one question: how do we help strong local organisations get through a donor's due diligence?

Donors increasingly want to put more funding in the hands of local and national organisations, directly or through sub-grants. The organisations doing the work are often more than capable. What holds them back is usually paperwork and systems, not the quality of their programmes. The good news is that most of it can be fixed with a plan.

What donors usually look at

Every donor and every INGO has its own assessment, but most cover the same ground:

  • Legal registration and governance. Proof that you're registered, your founding documents and a board or governing body that meets and provides real oversight.
  • Financial management. An accounting system, a bank account in the organisation's name, approval limits and separation of duties, so one person can't both approve and pay, and past audits or financial statements if you have them.
  • Core policies. Safeguarding and protection from sexual exploitation and abuse, anti-fraud and anti-corruption, conflict of interest, procurement, data protection and how you check partners and suppliers against sanctions lists.
  • Human resources. Contracts, job descriptions, payroll records and a code of conduct staff have signed.
  • Track record. Evidence of projects you've delivered, ideally including grants from other donors and the reports you sent them.

How to get ready

Start before there's a call

Due diligence done in the two weeks before a deadline is stressful and patchy. Do a self-assessment now, using a donor's or an INGO partner's checklist, so you know where the gaps are while there's time to close them.

Fix the biggest risks first

Not every gap matters equally. Weak financial controls and missing safeguarding arrangements worry donors far more than a policy that needs updating. Rank the gaps by risk and work through them in that order.

Write policies you'll actually use

It's tempting to copy a policy from a large INGO and change the name. Assessors often ask staff how a policy works in practice, and a document nobody has read won't survive that conversation. Keep policies short, fit them to your size and context, and train the people who need to use them.

Keep an evidence folder

Gather everything in one place: registration certificate, board minutes, audits, policies, organisation chart, past reports and references from donors. Keep it up to date and you'll be able to answer most due diligence requests in days rather than weeks.

Put the cost in the budget

Strong systems cost money: a finance officer's time, an annual audit, accounting software, training. INGOs and donors who want to work with local partners should budget for this, including a fair share of indirect costs. If you're the INGO writing the proposal, make sure your partners' costs are in it.

For INGOs: capacity strengthening works best as a partnership, not an inspection. Agree the plan together, share what you learn about your own systems, and recognise what your partners already do well.
