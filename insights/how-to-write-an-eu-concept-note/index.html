How to write an EU concept note that gets you to the full proposal

Kelly Tsanova30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Many EU calls for proposals run in two stages. First you send a short concept note. Only the applicants whose notes score well are invited to send a full application. That first stage is where many good organisations fall, usually not because their idea was weak but because the note didn't show the evaluators what they were looking for.

I've written and reviewed proposals for EU-funded work with DanChurchAid in Cambodia and MAG in Myanmar, alongside bids to many other institutional donors. These are the habits that make the difference.

1. Read the guidelines, then read the evaluation grid again

Every call comes with Guidelines for Applicants. They set out the objectives and priorities of the call, who is eligible, how much you can ask for and how the concept note will be scored. The evaluation grid is the most useful page in the whole pack. The person reading your note will have it open beside them, so write to it.

Before you draft anything, copy the grid's questions into your working document as headings. If a question asks how the action is relevant to the call's priorities, that question deserves a clear, direct answer, not a hint somewhere in paragraph four.

2. Follow the format to the letter

The guidelines fix the page limit and often the font and size too. Use the official template and keep its headings. Evaluators score a large pile of notes against the same grid, and anything that makes yours harder to compare works against you. A note that runs over the limit risks having the extra pages ignored, or being rejected outright.

3. Start with the problem, not with your organisation

It's tempting to open with your history and your achievements. Lead instead with the problem you want to solve, for whom and where, backed by evidence: recent data, assessments, your own monitoring or other studies. Then show how it links to the specific objectives of the call and, where relevant, to the EU's priorities for that country.

Your experience matters, but it belongs where the grid asks about it. In the opening, the evaluator wants to know why this action, here, now.

4. Be specific about who will benefit

The EU distinguishes between target groups, the people or organisations the action works with directly, and final beneficiaries, who benefit in the longer term. Name both. Say roughly how many, where they are and how they were involved in shaping the idea. "Vulnerable communities" on its own tells an evaluator very little. "Around 2,000 women farmers in four districts, consulted through our partner's savings groups" tells them a lot.

5. Show the logic in a few lines

You don't usually need a full logframe at concept note stage, but you need its spine. Make it easy to see how the activities lead to the outputs, how those lead to the outcomes and how the outcomes serve the overall objective. If you can't explain that chain in a short paragraph, the full proposal will struggle too.

Think the logic through properly now. If you're invited to the next stage, the full application has to stay consistent with what you promised in the concept note.

6. Give partners real roles

If you're applying with co-applicants or affiliated entities, explain why each one is there and what it will actually do. Many calls now place real weight on local ownership. A local partner listed only as a name, with no clear role or share of the budget, is easy for an evaluator to spot. Partners who helped design the action and will lead parts of it make a much stronger case.

7. Keep the money realistic

At concept note stage you often give only the amount you're requesting, but it still needs to make sense. Check that it falls between the call's minimum and maximum, that the scale of activities matches the amount and that you can cover any co-financing the call requires. An action that promises too much for the money is as unconvincing as one that asks for too much.

8. Sort out registration early

Most EU external action calls are now managed through the EU's Funding & Tenders Portal, and your organisation and any co-applicants need to be registered there. Registration and validation can take longer than you expect, so start it as soon as you decide to apply, not in the final week.

9. Get a fresh pair of eyes before you submit

When you've lived with a draft for weeks, you stop seeing what's missing. Ask someone who hasn't worked on it to score it against the evaluation grid. Check the eligibility criteria one last time, then submit a day or two early. Online portals slow down close to a deadline, and there's no second chance if an upload fails.

A quick self-check: could an evaluator, reading only your note, answer every question in the evaluation grid and find the answer quickly? If not, that's where to spend your next hour.
